## Generalization: Robustness to Irrelevant Preceding Context

We tested whether LoRA fine-tuning changes how strongly irrelevant context disrupts short-answer generation. Specifically, we asked whether the published seed-17, epoch-2 Qwen3-0.6B LoRA checkpoint loses less exact-match accuracy (EM) than its frozen base model when unrelated text is placed *before* the answer-bearing passage. Before the initial experiment, we predicted that adding approximately 1,024 tokens would reduce LoRA EM by at least 8 percentage points (pp) relative to the same questions without added text. This experiment tests robustness within SQuAD 1.1; it does not measure transfer to a new domain.

**Shared data and evaluation setup.** We used the existing MRQA 2019 SQuAD 1.1 subset and official SQuAD article information; we did not collect new examples. The published training pipeline removed exact and lexical near-duplicate records and fixed article-disjoint partitions before evaluation. The run used 2,048 training questions, 256 validation questions, and 900 evaluation questions from 45 articles held out from LoRA fine-tuning. The inference efficiency experiments used all 900 evaluation questions. Our initial generalization sample contained 90 questions, and the follow-up used its exact 810-question complement. Both generalization samples therefore come from the same 45 articles. The evaluation questions originate from a public development set, not a hidden test set; the follow-up does not test transfer to new articles or domains.

The initial stress test selected two questions per article (90 total) deterministically and evaluated original context, +512 tokens, and +1,024 tokens. The added text came from other test articles; each length used two fixed distractor seeds. After examining the initial results, we froze a follow-up plan for the remaining 810 questions (18 more per article), using only original context and +1,024 tokens with the same seeds. Both models received identical question–context inputs and the published prompt. Decoding was greedy with a 64-new-token limit; no further training occurred and no evaluated input was truncated. For the follow-up, our primary estimand was the LoRA EM change minus the base-model EM change on matched questions. We averaged the two distractor seeds within each question and used 2,000 bootstrap resamples of the 45 articles to form 95% percentile intervals.

| Sample | Model | Original EM | +1,024 EM | Paired change |
|---|---|---:|---:|---:|
| Initial 90 | Base | 20.00% | 12.22% | −7.78 pp |
| Initial 90 | LoRA | 78.89% | 76.67% | −2.22 pp |
| Remaining 810 | Base | 22.96% | 14.07% | −8.89 pp |
| Remaining 810 | LoRA | 76.79% | 71.30% | −5.49 pp |

Values at +1,024 tokens average the two fixed distractor seeds. In the initial 90 questions, LoRA's EM change was −2.22 pp (95% article-cluster interval −8.89 to +3.89 pp); thus, the prespecified 8-point decline was **not met by the point estimate**, although the interval remained wide. The exploratory paired difference between LoRA and base-model changes was +5.56 pp (95% interval −3.89 to +15.56 pp), which did not establish a relative robustness advantage. In the 810-question follow-up, the corresponding difference was **+3.40 pp** (95% interval **+0.93 to +5.80 pp**), with the lower bound above zero. The secondary token-F1 difference in changes was +3.18 pp (95% interval +0.78 to +5.50 pp). These results support the narrower conclusion that this LoRA checkpoint had a smaller EM decline than the base model in the tested SQuAD preceding-context setting. They do not change the outcome of the original 8-point prediction.

We also examined LoRA responses that were EM-correct with the original passage but incorrect after adding 1,024 tokens. There were 149 seed-specific loss records involving 104 distinct questions. An exact normalized substring check located the wrong prediction only in the original passage in 138 records, only in the distractor in 2, in both in 2, and in neither in 7. For example, one ABC answer changed from “The Dating Game” to “The Newlywed Game,” another title in the original passage, whereas one Luther answer changed from “Johann Tetzel” to “Trotsky,” a distractor-only span. In 41 loss records, the incorrect EM output still contained the originally correct prediction, often as an overlong answer. These string locations identify where text appears, not why the model selected it; “neither” does not by itself indicate hallucination.

The 810 follow-up questions were new questions from the **same 45 articles**, and only two distractor seeds and one trained LoRA checkpoint were evaluated. Added length and distractor content also change together. Consequently, the evidence supports relative robustness to this particular preceding-context perturbation, but not transfer to unseen articles, domains, arbitrary distractors, or other training seeds. Prediction-level data, run manifests, code, and checksum-verified reproduction instructions are included in the generalization supplementary bundle.
